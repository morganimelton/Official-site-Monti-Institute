How to Find Evidence-Based Migraine Care?

By Editorial Team

Evidence-based migraine care starts with a correct diagnosis. Learn how to vet clinicians, track symptoms and ask the questions that matter.

Quick Summary

Evidence-based migraine care means treatment guided by clinical research and professional guidelines, then adjusted to your own history. Start with a confirmed diagnosis, keep a headache diary, and verify any specialist's licensing and board certification before you commit.

  • Confirm the migraine diagnosis first, since other conditions can cause similar headaches.
  • Track headache days, pain severity, triggers and medication use in a diary for two to four weeks before your first visit.
  • Check a clinician's license through your state medical board and board certification through the issuing board.
  • Ask what a recommendation is based on, how strong the evidence is, and what benefit to expect.
  • Decide in advance how you will measure success, such as headache days per month, and review it at each follow-up.

What Evidence-Based Migraine Care Actually Means

Last Updated: November 26, 2026

Finding evidence-based migraine care starts with knowing what that phrase means. Evidence-based care follows treatment recommendations supported by clinical research and professional guidelines, and it adjusts those recommendations to the individual patient.

Migraine is a neurological condition. It causes repeated attacks of moderate to severe head pain, often with nausea, light sensitivity, or sound sensitivity.

Evidence-based care does not mean one treatment for everyone. It means a clinician:

  • Confirms the diagnosis before recommending treatment
  • Uses treatments with documented benefit for migraine
  • Explains the expected benefits, side effects, and limits of each option
  • Reviews whether treatment is working and adjusts the plan

Watch out: Many patients try several treatments without tracking results. Without a record, it becomes hard to tell whether a treatment helped or whether attacks changed on their own.

When you evaluate any clinic, ask how it decides what to recommend.

American Headache Society migraine treatment guidelines

Step 1: Get a Proper Migraine Diagnosis

A proper diagnosis is the foundation of evidence-based care. Headache disorders have many causes, and some symptoms need urgent evaluation. A clinician should rule out other conditions before settling on migraine.

During a diagnostic visit, expect the clinician to ask about:

  • How often headaches happen and how long they last
  • Where the pain sits and how it feels
  • Symptoms like nausea, light sensitivity, or aura
  • Any family history of migraine
  • Current medications, including over-the-counter pain relievers

Keep in mind that frequent use of pain relievers can cause medication overuse headache. Tell your clinician how often you take them, not just which ones you use.

Red flags that need prompt medical attention:

  • A sudden, severe headache that feels like the worst of your life
  • Headache with fever, stiff neck, confusion, or weakness
  • New headaches after age 50 or a change in your usual pattern
  • Headache with vision loss, trouble speaking, or numbness on one side
  • Headache after a head injury

If you have any of these, seek emergency care instead of waiting for a routine appointment.

Step 2: Start a Migraine Diary Before Your First Visit

A headache diary gives your clinician the information they need most. Start tracking at least two to four weeks before your first visit if you can.

Record for each headache day:

  • Date and time the headache started
  • Pain severity on a 0 to 10 scale
  • How long it lasted
  • Symptoms, such as nausea or light sensitivity
  • Medication taken and whether it helped
  • Possible triggers, such as poor sleep, skipped meals, stress, or certain foods
  • Activities that day, including exercise and screen time

Use a paper notebook or a phone app. Bring the diary to your appointment. A simple count of headache days per month is often more useful than a long narrative.

Watch out: Do not guess at triggers. Note what you noticed, then let the clinician help you judge what matters.

Step 3: Find and Verify a Qualified Migraine Specialist

Many people with migraine see a primary care clinician first, and that is often appropriate.

American Migraine Foundation

Where to look

  • Start with your primary care clinician and ask for a referral to a neurologist or headache specialist, and ask why that particular clinician is being recommended.
  • Use your health plan's provider directory to filter by neurology and headache medicine, then confirm the listing is current by calling the practice.
  • Check the American Headache Society and the National Headache Foundation for clinician-finding resources and for information on headache medicine training.
  • If you live far from a specialist, ask whether the practice offers telehealth consultations for follow-up visits; many do, and follow-up is where most treatment adjustment happens.

How to verify a clinician's credentials

  • Confirm the license through your state medical board's online lookup. The license should be active and free of public disciplinary actions you would want to know about.
  • Ask whether the clinician is board certified in neurology or in headache medicine, and confirm certification through the issuing board. The American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology maintains a certification search, and the United Council for Neurologic Subspecialties certifies headache medicine subspecialists.
  • Ask how much of the clinician's practice is devoted to headache care. A neurologist who sees headache patients daily will usually have more experience with treatment sequencing than a generalist who sees them occasionally.
  • Ask whether the practice participates in headache research, follows published guidelines, or is affiliated with an academic center. None of these is required, but each is a signal of engagement with current evidence.

American Board of Psychiatry and Neurology certification search

Questions to ask before you book

  • Who will actually see me at each visit, and will I see the same clinician over time?
  • How does the practice handle medication adjustments between visits, including refills and side-effect concerns?
  • What is the follow-up schedule, and how are results reviewed?
  • What is the expected cost, and is the practice in network with your insurance?
  • Does the practice offer behavioral therapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy or biofeedback, or refer to clinicians who do?

How to prepare for the first visit

Bring your headache diary, a complete medication list including over-the-counter products and supplements, prior treatment records, and imaging or lab results you already have.

Step 4: Evaluate Acute and Preventive Treatment Options

Treatment choices depend on how often you have attacks, how severe they are, how disabling they are, and your health history. The skill to build here is not memorizing drug names; it is learning to ask what the evidence shows, how strong it is, and how it applies to you. (Source: National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke)

Acute treatments are used when an attack starts. Common options include:

  • Over-the-counter pain relievers such as NSAIDs or acetaminophen, taken early in an attack
  • Prescription migraine-specific medications, such as triptans, which are most effective when taken early in an attack
  • Gepants, a newer class of acute migraine medications, for some patients
  • Anti-nausea medication when nausea is severe

Preventive treatments are used on a schedule, usually when attacks are frequent or disabling. Options that clinicians commonly consider include:

  • Oral medications from several classes, including beta-blockers, certain antidepressants, and certain anti-seizure medications
  • CGRP-targeted therapies, given as injections or infusions, for some patients
  • Botulinum toxin injections for chronic migraine, which is generally defined as headache on 15 or more days per month with migraine features on at least 8 of those days
  • Other therapies your clinician judges appropriate for your history

Preventive care often takes several weeks to show an effect, and the trial period matters. Ask how long a fair trial is for the specific option before judging it.

Non-pharmacological options can support medication.

How to judge the evidence behind a recommendation

Clinical guidelines grade recommendations by the strength of the underlying evidence, and the grade tells you how much confidence to place in a recommendation. A strong recommendation backed by high-quality evidence means the benefits clearly outweigh the risks for most patients. A weaker recommendation may still be reasonable, but it usually means the evidence is less certain, the benefit is smaller, or the choice depends more on patient preference. When your clinician recommends a treatment, ask:

  • What is this recommendation based on, and how strong is the evidence?
  • What benefit should I expect, and how was that benefit measured in studies?
  • What are the common and serious side effects, and what would make this option unsafe for me?
  • What are the alternatives, including doing nothing or trying a non-drug approach first?
  • How long should I try this before we decide whether it is working?

Weighing benefits, risks, and your circumstances

Evidence-based care is not the same as one-size-fits-all care. The same recommendation can be right for one patient and wrong for another. Factors that should shape the decision include:

  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding, or plans to become pregnant, which change which medications are appropriate
  • Other medical conditions, such as heart disease, high blood pressure, kidney disease, or liver disease, that make some options unsafe
  • Other medications and supplements, because interactions can matter more than the migraine treatment itself
  • How often you have attacks and how much they disrupt work, school, caregiving, or sleep
  • Your preferences about daily pills versus occasional acute treatment, injections versus oral options, and how much side-effect risk you are willing to accept

Shared decision-making is the process of working through these factors with your clinician rather than having a plan handed to you.

Watch out: Guidelines sometimes differ in their recommendations, and a recommendation that is strong in one guideline may be more cautious in another.

Step 5: Judge Whether Your Treatment Is Working

Decide in advance how you will measure success. Useful measures include:

  • Change in headache days per month
  • Change in pain severity and how long attacks last
  • Days missed from work, school, or daily activities
  • Number of acute medication doses needed each month
  • Side effects you notice

Review your diary at each follow-up. Improvement can be gradual, so give preventive treatments enough time to show an effect before changing plans.

Keep track of how often you take acute medication.

Watch out: Stopping a medication suddenly without medical advice can cause problems. Make changes with your clinician.

Shared decision-making matters here.

Tracking a few measures over time makes it easier to tell whether a treatment is helping.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is migraine diagnosed?

Migraine is diagnosed mainly from your medical history and a clinical assessment, not from a single scan or blood test. A clinician asks about headache frequency, duration, location, associated symptoms such as nausea or light sensitivity, and what helps or worsens the pain. A neurological exam is usually done to rule out other causes. Imaging is ordered only when there are red flags, such as a sudden severe headache, new neurological symptoms, or a change in pattern after age 50. Bringing a headache diary to the first visit makes the diagnosis more accurate.

What questions should I ask a doctor about migraine treatment?

Ask what diagnosis they have reached and why, which acute treatments are recommended for your attacks, and whether a preventive treatment makes sense for your attack frequency. Ask about expected benefits, common side effects, and how long you should try a treatment before judging it. Ask how medication overuse is avoided, since taking pain relievers too often can make headaches more frequent. Also ask what they will do if the first treatment does not work, and how follow-up will be scheduled.

How can I tell whether a migraine treatment is working?

Compare your headache diary from before treatment with entries kept during it. Look at attack frequency, headache days per month, pain severity, and how much the attacks disrupt work or sleep. Improvement is usually measured over two to three months for a preventive treatment, while acute medication should reliably ease individual attacks when taken early. If your diary shows no change, or the treatment causes side effects you cannot tolerate, discuss adjusting the plan with your clinician rather than stopping on your own.
